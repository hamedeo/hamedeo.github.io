### *When Exclusion Becomes Identity*

When we say, “I was rejected because I am Iranian,” we may be describing a real experience of discrimination. But we are also allowing an institution’s decision to sound like a natural consequence of who we are.

I have used this language myself in the past. Yet it performs a subtle transformation: governments, regulations, employers and managers disappear from the sentence, while “Iranian” remains as though it were an inherent professional limitation. Something imposed on us begins to sound like something wrong with us.

> **When we remove those decision-makers from the sentence, the exclusion remains, but the people responsible for it disappear.**

Karen E. Fields and Barbara J. Fields describe a similar process in *Racecraft*. They examine the sentence:

“Black Southerners were segregated because of their skin color.”

The sentence appears descriptive, but its grammar performs a subtle trick. The segregationists disappear. Segregation, something imposed through laws and human decisions, reappears as a consequence of the people’s skin color. Racism, something an actor does, is transformed into race, something the target supposedly is.[^1]

![Bahman Mohassess's Il Minotauro fa Paura alla Gente per Bene, depicting a dark minotaur-like figure](./Pics/Il-Minotauro-fa-Paura-alla-Gente-per-Bene.jpeg "class\:article-image article-image--full")

<small class="article-image-credit">When identity is made to signify danger, exclusion begins before the individual is known. Bahman Mohassess, *Il Minotauro fa Paura alla Gente per Bene*, 1966.</small>

Race is not a natural fact waiting to be discovered. It is produced when institutions repeatedly treat a human distinction as though it reveals something inherent about the people placed within it. In this case, Iranian nationality is transformed into an imagined measure of loyalty, security or professional suitability. That is how nationality is made to function as race.

I have noticed a similar pattern in the way some Iranians describe obstacles in employment. A recruitment process stops after nationality is discussed. A company refers vaguely to “American technology” or “export controls.”[^2] We then say:

“I was rejected because I am Iranian.”

Or, more broadly:

“They do not hire Iranians.”

The frustration behind these statements is real. Some positions involve technology whose release to Iranian nationals requires regulatory approval. Yet a restriction on access to particular technology is not the same as a prohibition on employing Iranian people.[^2] Corporate policy, the company’s willingness to seek a license, its ability to separate controlled work and its tolerance for administrative complexity may all affect the decision.

When we say only, “I was rejected because I am Iranian,” Iranian nationality appears to have caused the rejection. The decision-makers, regulations and corporate choices disappear. “Iranian” gradually begins to sound like an inherent professional limitation rather than the object of a policy imposed by others.

This does not mean that Iranians create their own discrimination. Nor does changing our vocabulary make the exclusion less real. Responsibility belongs to those who establish and apply the unequal rule.

But language can reveal that process or conceal it.

We should first ask the employer to identify the specific restriction. When describing the experience, instead of saying, “I was rejected because I am Iranian,” you can say:

“The hiring team appears to have excluded me based on its interpretation of the company’s export-control policy.”

Nationality screening may satisfy a specific export-control obligation, but it should not be mistaken for a complete security assessment. Sensitive information can be compromised by people of any nationality, so effective protection also requires controlled access, monitoring and individual accountability.[^3]

> **Iranian citizens are not extensions of the Iranian government.**

In fact, many are among those most directly harmed by its decisions.[^4] When restrictions abroad impose an additional burden on them, we should name that injustice without allowing “Iranian” to become an explanation for it.

## When Compliance Becomes Pre-emptive Exclusion

In the Netherlands, nationality is a protected ground, and employers generally may not exclude applicants merely because of their passport.[^5] Export controls may restrict access to specific technologies, but the restriction must be demonstrated for the actual position. Dutch decisions distinguish between a substantiated legal obstacle and rejection based only on assumptions about nationality.[^6]

The problem becomes systemic when a narrow exception turns into a general hiring shortcut. In my experience, Iranian candidates across high-tech employment, secondment and sensitive research may be treated as less deployable because a present or future client might object. Managers can then exclude them before anyone has established that a restriction applies to the actual position. Anticipated exclusion becomes exclusion in practice.[^7]

![Diagram showing a narrow compliance clause expanding into broad gatekeeping based on nationality](./Pics/Exclusion-and-Nationality.png "class\:article-image article-image--full")

<small class="article-image-credit">When a narrow compliance clause becomes a broader gatekeeping tool.</small>

Once normalized, a blanket nationality restriction can extend far beyond the position for which it was introduced. Managers, contractors and secondment agencies may apply it pre-emptively to avoid possible future complications, even when no restriction has been established for the actual role. A narrow compliance obligation can therefore become a wider pattern of exclusion.

This also makes recruitment unequal. Other candidates can ask, “What work do I want to do?” Iranian candidates are often left asking, “What work am I permitted to do?”—without being told which roles are restricted, which rule applies or whether alternatives exist. Applying the same HR procedure to everyone is not necessarily fair when one group must navigate additional institutional barriers.

Organizations working with controlled technology should therefore provide a transparent compliance pathway. They should identify possible restrictions early, assess each role individually, and consider licensing, limited access or alternative responsibilities before rejecting a candidate.[^8] Export-control guidance itself recommends integrating these assessments into recruitment and HR procedures.

Changing our language does not remove the discrimination. It keeps the actor and decision visible.

**We are not rejected by our nationality. We are excluded by decisions made about it—and those decisions must remain visible and open to challenge.**

The guidance addresses license applications, technology-control plans and internal access controls for foreign-national employees.
